# Netherlands clues

Entries use the shared format (`references/clues/README.md`). Use these only after the Netherlands is a candidate; cross-country clues belong in `references/clues/global.md`. Sources checked on 2026-10-31. The pack is a stub: clues only, no admin table or lookup tables yet.

## Text and plates

### Dutch spelling on fixed signs
- Look for: Dutch words on shop fronts, street plates and notices: `Uitgang`, `Ingang`, `Apotheek`, `Bakkerij`, `Gemeente`, `Fietsers`, `Let op`.
- Points to: the Netherlands (country-level confirmation); the doubled vowels and `ij` digraph are distinctive.
- Strength: strong for the country; weak for any province.
- Counterexamples: Belgium (Flanders) uses the same language with slightly different vocabulary (`apotheek` is shared, but `gemeente`/`gemeentehuis` and road wording differ), so a single word does not separate the two. English-only signage is common in Amsterdam and in tourist areas.
- Sources: [Wikipedia: Dutch language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_language) covers the varieties; [government.nl](https://www.government.nl/) is the official portal.

### Yellow plate with a blue EU band
- Look for: a yellow plate with black characters, a blue band with the EU stars and `NL` on the left, and the format `AA-99-BB` (or `99-AA-BB` for older series). The plate carries no region code.
- Points to: the Netherlands as the issuing country; the yellow colour separates it from Belgium's white plates and Germany's white plates.
- Strength: strong for the country (single source); useless for a province.
- Counterexamples: Luxembourg and the UK also use yellow rear plates, and taxis or trailers can carry other formats. A rental car with a foreign plate says nothing about the photo's location.
- Sources: [Wikipedia: Vehicle registration plates of the Netherlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vehicle_registration_plates_of_the_Netherlands) documents the yellow plate and the sidecodes.

## Addresses and numbers

### Four digits, a space, two capitals
- Look for: a postal code written `1234 AB` before the city name, for example `1012 JS Amsterdam` or `3011 BD Rotterdam`, on mailboxes, vans and shop windows.
- Points to: the Netherlands; the four digits identify a street or part of one, and the two letters a small group of addresses.
- Strength: medium (single source); the code names a delivery area, not a building.
- Counterexamples: Belgium uses four digits without letters, and Germany five digits. A code can be printed on a mail piece handled elsewhere.
- Sources: [PostNL](https://www.postnl.nl/) publishes the postal-code format and directory.

### Area codes that start with zero
- Look for: phone numbers written with a leading zero for the area code (`010` Rotterdam, `020` Amsterdam, `030` Utrecht, `040` Eindhoven, `070` The Hague) or `06` for mobiles, often with `+31` abroad.
- Points to: the Netherlands; the leading zero is dropped only when the country code is used.
- Strength: medium (single source); the two-digit codes name the largest cities, three-digit codes smaller regions.
- Counterexamples: Belgium also uses a leading zero (`02` Brussels, `03` Antwerp) with nine digits in total; only the `+31` or the code itself separates them.
- Sources: [ACM](https://www.acm.nl/) is the regulator that publishes the numbering plan.

## Streets and transport

### Red cycle paths and bike-first junctions
- Look for: smooth red asphalt cycle paths beside the road, separate traffic-light heads for bikes, and roundabouts where cyclists have priority; `fietspad` signs and painted bicycle symbols.
- Points to: the Netherlands; the density and paint colour of cycle infrastructure is distinctive, though Belgium also builds red cycle paths.
- Strength: medium; corroborate with language or plates.
- Counterexamples: Belgium and Denmark have extensive cycling infrastructure too, and red asphalt appears in both. Newer Dutch paths can be grey or black.
- Sources: [Wikipedia: Cycling in the Netherlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cycling_in_the_Netherlands) describes the network and its markings.

### Yellow-and-blue NS trains and the supermarket chains
- Look for: trains in the yellow-and-blue `NS` livery, or the chains `Albert Heijn`, `Jumbo` and `HEMA` on shop fronts and delivery vans.
- Points to: the Netherlands; the chains are Dutch and their branding is everywhere, including small towns.
- Strength: medium (single source); the brands confirm the country but not the province.
- Counterexamples: AH and Jumbo have Belgian and German stores in border regions, and NS trains run to Brussels. Franchise holders can use the brand outside its home market.
- Sources: [NS](https://www.ns.nl/) is the national rail operator; [Albert Heijn](https://www.ah.nl/) and [Jumbo](https://www.jumbo.com/) publish their store networks.
