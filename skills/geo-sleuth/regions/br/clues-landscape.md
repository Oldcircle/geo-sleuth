# Brazil clues: vegetation, soil and crops

Entries use the shared format (`references/clues/README.md`). Vegetation without a known month is weak evidence on its own: the Cerrado and the Caatinga change colour completely between the wet and the dry season. State shares come from IBGE (biome map, PAM, PEVS) unless another source is named, and each says whether it is a share of area, output or wild harvest. In the Strength lines, "a second clue" means one that is not vegetation or soil (a plate, road number, distributor, shop chain or text, see clues.md), or a known month where the entry says so.

## Trees, palms and biomes

### Araucária (Paraná pine)
- Look for: a tall conifer with a long bare trunk and a flat-topped crown of upturned branches carrying foliage only at their tips (a candelabra or cup); young trees are plain cones
- Points to: the southern highlands of Paraná, Santa Catarina and Rio Grande do Sul, plus scattered stands in southern São Paulo, usually above 500 m and typically around 1,000 m; smaller patches in the Serra da Mantiqueira (southern Minas Gerais, eastern São Paulo), on high ground in Rio de Janeiro and, very rarely, in Espírito Santo (a relict in the Serra do Caparaó)
- Strength: weak alone, like any vegetation; medium for mature stands on a plateau together with a second southern clue
- Counterexamples: valued as an ornamental and planted outside its natural range (one planting is recorded near Ubaíra in southern Bahia, single source), so a single tree in a garden or square proves little; young trees look like any conifer; small relict patches also grow in north-eastern Misiones (Argentina) and in Alto Paraná (Paraguay) (single source)
- Sources: Embrapa, Carvalho 2002 (original range by state including southern São Paulo, the Caparaó relict and the Misiones and Alto Paraná patches, crown shape, ornamental use, the Ubaíra planting), https://www.infoteca.cnptia.embrapa.br/bitstream/doc/304455/1/CT0060.pdf; Embrapa, Wrege et al. 2017 (altitude and latitude limits, very rare in Rio de Janeiro and Espírito Santo), https://pfb.sede.embrapa.br/pfb/article/download/1413/592/16324; IBGE, Biomas e Sistema Costeiro-Marinho do Brasil, 2019 (Araucaria forest from southern São Paulo to Rio Grande do Sul, Mantiqueira outliers), https://biblioteca.ibge.gov.br/visualizacao/livros/liv101676.pdf; Flora e Funga do Brasil via GBIF (native in MG, PR, RJ, RS, SC and SP, accessed Nov 2026), https://api.gbif.org/v1/species/114810361/distributions; Plonkit Brazil guide (community, mainly PR, SC and RS, updated 31 Oct 2026), https://www.plonkit.net/brazil

### Cerrado savanna
- Look for: a grass layer with scattered low (3–10 m), leaning, twisted trees with thick, cracked, corky bark and stiff leaves, often with fire scars; the ground layer grows lush in the rains (about October–April), and the dry season runs about May–September
- Points to: the centre of the country: all of the Distrito Federal and nearly all of Goiás and Tocantins; half or more of Maranhão, Mato Grosso do Sul, Minas Gerais and Piauí; nearly two-fifths (37%) of Mato Grosso; western Bahia and parts of São Paulo (IBGE moved more of São Paulo into the Cerrado in 2025)
- Strength: weak (vegetation; the look changes with the season)
- Counterexamples: savanna enclaves inside the Amazon (Roraima, northern Pará, Amapá, Marajó, southern Amazonas, Rondônia) and inside the Atlantic Forest zone; the Pantanal is largely savanna too
- Sources: IBGE, Biomas e Sistema Costeiro-Marinho do Brasil, 2019 (biome share per state, savanna structure, savanna enclaves), https://biblioteca.ibge.gov.br/visualizacao/livros/liv101676.pdf; Embrapa, Ribeiro & Walter 2008, Cerrado physiognomies (low, leaning, twisted trees with thick corky bark, ground layer lush in the rains), https://www.alice.cnptia.embrapa.br/alice/bitstream/doc/186922/1/CERRADO-Ecologia-e-flora-VOL-1-152-213.pdf; Embrapa, Althoff et al. 2024, O clima do Cerrado (dry May–September, rains October–April), https://www.infoteca.cnptia.embrapa.br/infoteca/handle/doc/1165575; en.wikipedia "Cerrado" (winter dry season, tree heights by physiognomy, accessed Nov 2026), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerrado; Monitor Mercantil on IBGE's boundary revision (Cerrado enlarged mainly in centre-north São Paulo, 18 Nov 2025), https://monitormercantil.com.br/ibge-revisa-limites-e-cerrado-ganha-area-enquanto-mata-atlantica-perde-1/; IBGE, Quadro Geográfico biome sheet 2025 (2025 revision of the Atlantic Forest–Cerrado limit, the first since the 2019 map), https://www.ibge.gov.br/apps/quadrogeografico/pdf/qg_2025_240_bioma.pdf

### Caatinga with columnar cacti
- Look for: low, thorny, deciduous scrub, grey and leafless in the dry season and briefly bright green after rain, with columnar cacti (mandacaru, xique-xique) and stony ground
- Points to: the semi-arid North-east: all of Ceará and nearly all of Rio Grande do Norte and Paraíba, most of Pernambuco, about 60% of Bahia, half of Sergipe, Alagoas and Piauí, and a sliver of northern Minas Gerais; not Maranhão
- Strength: weak on its own (vegetation, month unknown); medium with columnar cacti in view plus a known month that fits the scrub's state, or with a second North-east clue
- Counterexamples: similar dry scrub grows at Cabo Frio (Rio de Janeiro), in north-eastern Minas Gerais, northern Roraima and the southern, Chaco-like Pantanal; in the rains the scrub turns green but keeps its low, thorny, many-stemmed build (the rains run about February–May in Ceará and November–April around Petrolina–Juazeiro, single source each); mandacaru also grows in the Cerrado, on rock outcrops and on disturbed ground, and other columnar cacti are native outside the semi-arid, such as Cereus hildmannianus in the Atlantic Forest and Pampa from Minas Gerais and Rio de Janeiro to Rio Grande do Sul and Pilosocereus arrabidae in the Atlantic Forest of Bahia, Espírito Santo and Rio de Janeiro (single source); the official "semi-arid" region (a policy area of 1,477 municipalities) also takes in parts of Maranhão and Espírito Santo

### Buriti palms in a wet valley (vereda)
- Look for: groups of tall (12–15 m) fan palms with round leaves, the lower ones dead and hanging, standing in a wet grassy valley bottom
- Points to: the Cerrado and the Amazon; buriti is native in 16 states plus the Distrito Federal, from São Paulo and Mato Grosso do Sul north to Roraima and Amapá (Flora e Funga lists 14 states plus the DF, and Embrapa adds Amapá and Roraima); neither list includes the South, Espírito Santo, Rio de Janeiro or the states from Rio Grande do Norte to Sergipe, so it mainly helps rule those out
- Strength: weak (useful to exclude the South)
- Counterexamples: the range is wide; stray clusters turn up far from the core range (single source)
- Sources: IBGE, Biomas e Sistema Costeiro-Marinho do Brasil, 2019 (veredas: buriti on waterlogged valley soils across the Cerrado), https://biblioteca.ibge.gov.br/visualizacao/livros/liv101676.pdf; Embrapa, Ribeiro & Walter 2008 (vereda structure, adult buritis 12–15 m), https://www.alice.cnptia.embrapa.br/alice/bitstream/doc/186922/1/CERRADO-Ecologia-e-flora-VOL-1-152-213.pdf; Flora e Funga do Brasil via GBIF (native in AC, AM, BA, CE, DF, GO, MA, MG, MS, MT, PA, PI, RO, SP and TO, accessed Nov 2026), https://api.gbif.org/v1/species/114652360/distributions; Embrapa, Palmeiras Nativas do Brasil 2015, Buriti chapter (large areas of 16 states plus the DF, Amapá and Roraima included, on flooded or poorly drained ground), https://www.alice.cnptia.embrapa.br/alice/bitstream/doc/1019828/1/Buriti.pdf; Plonkit Brazil guide (community, dead hanging lower leaves, most common in RR, AM, TO and GO, stray clusters far away, updated 31 Oct 2026), https://www.plonkit.net/brazil

### Carnaúba palm stands
- Look for: stands of fan palms on flat, seasonally flooded river plains; compact, rigid fans, and a lower trunk sleeved in the stubs of old leaf bases below a bare upper trunk
- Points to: Piauí and Ceará, then Rio Grande do Norte (nearly all carnaúba wax and powder comes from Ceará and Piauí)
- Strength: weak on its own; medium for dense stands on a flat river plain together with a second North-east clue
- Counterexamples: the palm is native as far as Maranhão, Tocantins and Mato Grosso; stands also grow around Xique-Xique in Bahia, which gives about 7% of the wax; carandá, a close relative, forms similar dense stands on the Pantanal's flooded plains (Mato Grosso do Sul, Mato Grosso), though its fans are smaller (40–70 cm against about 1.5 m, single source)
- Sources: IBGE, PEVS 2025, wild harvest by state (Bahia about 7% of the wax), https://sidra.ibge.gov.br/tabela/289; Flora e Funga do Brasil via GBIF (native in AL, BA, CE, MA, MT, PB, PE, PI, RN, SE and TO, accessed Nov 2026), https://api.gbif.org/v1/species/114630207/distributions; en.wikipedia "Copernicia prunifera" (stands in flood zones and along rivers, Bahia among the main native states, fans about 1.5 m, accessed Nov 2026), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copernicia_prunifera; en.wikipedia "Copernicia alba" (carandá often forms dense single-species stands, leaves 40–70 cm, accessed Nov 2026), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copernicia_alba; IBGE, Biomas e Sistema Costeiro-Marinho do Brasil, 2019 (carandá common where water collects on saline Pantanal soils), https://biblioteca.ibge.gov.br/visualizacao/livros/liv101676.pdf; Plonkit Brazil guide (community, common in CE, PI and RN and around Xique-Xique in Bahia, updated 31 Oct 2026), https://www.plonkit.net/brazil

### Babaçu palm stands
- Look for: dense stands of tall, single-trunked feather palms with very long (5–13 m), erect, spreading fronds whose leaflets lie in one flat plane, in pasture or regrowth on cleared land (the palm survives fire and spreads after clearing)
- Points to: Maranhão above all (the Mata dos Cocais; the dense stands lie mainly in and around Maranhão, which gathers 87% of the kernels), then Piauí and Tocantins
- Strength: weak on its own; medium for dense stands in pasture together with a second Maranhão or Piauí clue
- Counterexamples: the palm ranges along the eastern and southern fringe of the Amazon (Embrapa lists every northern state plus Maranhão, Piauí, Ceará and Mato Grosso, and Flora e Funga lists 13 states), takes over wherever that forest is cleared and is a weed in Cerrado pastures, so the 87% shows where kernels are gathered, not where stands grow; macaúba, common around Belo Horizonte, Mato Grosso do Sul and northern Tocantins, also takes over cleared land, but its trunk bristles with long black spines and its fronds are shorter (3–4 m) with thin leaflets; the very similar Attalea vitrivir stands in pastures of northern Minas Gerais and neighbouring Bahia (single source); the community guide also reports babaçu, more rarely, down the east coast (single source)
- Sources: IBGE, PEVS 2025, wild harvest by state (kernels), https://sidra.ibge.gov.br/tabela/289; IBGE, Biomas e Sistema Costeiro-Marinho do Brasil, 2019 (the Mata dos Cocais babaçu forests of Maranhão), https://biblioteca.ibge.gov.br/visualizacao/livros/liv101676.pdf; Embrapa, Palmeiras Nativas do Brasil 2015, Babaçu chapter (fronds 5–13 m with leaflets in one plane, dense secondary stands of 13–18 million ha mainly in and around Maranhão, range along the Amazon fringe and spread into cleared forest, A. vitrivir in Minas Gerais and Bahia pastures), https://www.alice.cnptia.embrapa.br/alice/bitstream/doc/1050840/1/Babacu.pdf; Embrapa, Ribeiro & Walter 2008 (babaçu and macaúba stands resist fire and colonise cleared land), https://www.alice.cnptia.embrapa.br/alice/bitstream/doc/186922/1/CERRADO-Ecologia-e-flora-VOL-1-152-213.pdf; en.wikipedia "Attalea speciosa" (native to the Amazon region, a weed in Cerrado pastures, accessed Nov 2026), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attalea_speciosa; en.wikipedia "Acrocomia aculeata" (macaúba: spiny trunk, leaves 3–4 m with slender leaflets, accessed Nov 2026), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acrocomia_aculeata; Flora e Funga do Brasil via GBIF (native in 13 states, accessed Nov 2026), https://api.gbif.org/v1/species/114630917/distributions; Plonkit Brazil guide (community, long robust leaves, found across the Amazon, macaw palms around MS, northern TO and Belo Horizonte, updated 31 Oct 2026), https://www.plonkit.net/brazil

### Açaí palm clumps
- Look for: clumps of several very thin stems with drooping leaflets, in floodplain forest along rivers or in plantations on dry ground
- Points to: Pará, especially around Belém (Pará has about 95% of the farmed açaí area, 93% of farmed output and two-thirds of the wild harvest), then Maranhão and Amapá, where the clumping species is also native
- Strength: weak on its own; medium for dense clumps on a river floodplain together with a second Pará clue
- Counterexamples: much of the açaí of Amazonas (23% of the wild harvest) is the single-stemmed açaí-do-amazonas (Euterpe precatoria, native from Pará west to Acre, Rondônia and Roraima), so a lone thin trunk is weak evidence for Pará; similar slender palms occur rarely on the east coast from Santa Catarina to Bahia (single source), and the related juçara (Euterpe edulis) is native from Rio Grande do Sul to Bahia and inland to Goiás and Mato Grosso do Sul; açaí is also farmed in Bahia (about 1,700 ha in 2025)
- Sources: IBGE, PAM 2025, planted area and output by state (including Bahia's 1,691 ha), https://sidra.ibge.gov.br/tabela/5457; IBGE, PEVS 2025, wild harvest by state, https://sidra.ibge.gov.br/tabela/289; Embrapa, Farias Neto 2025, açaí chapter (E. oleracea grows in clumps, E. precatoria is single-stemmed and native in AC, AM, PA, RO and RR, floodplain stands and dry-ground plantings), https://www.alice.cnptia.embrapa.br/alice/bitstream/doc/1174949/1/CPAC-2025-Fruticultura-tropical-Acai.pdf; Embrapa Amazônia Ocidental, Circular Técnica 94, Nov 2025 (Amazonas grows açaí-do-amazonas, E. precatoria, mostly from wild stands), https://www.infoteca.cnptia.embrapa.br/infoteca/bitstream/doc/1182151/1/CircTec94.pdf; Flora e Funga do Brasil via GBIF (E. oleracea native in AP, MA, PA and TO, accessed Nov 2026), https://api.gbif.org/v1/species/114630964/distributions; Flora e Funga via GBIF (E. precatoria native in AC, AM, PA, RO and RR, accessed Nov 2026), https://api.gbif.org/v1/species/114690509/distributions; Flora e Funga via GBIF (E. edulis native in BA, DF, ES, GO, MG, MS, PR, RJ, RS, SC and SP, accessed Nov 2026), https://api.gbif.org/v1/species/114630968/distributions; Plonkit Brazil guide (community, most near Belém, drooping leaflets and thin trunks, rare lookalikes from SC to BA, updated 31 Oct 2026), https://www.plonkit.net/brazil

### Coconut palms
- Look for: tall palms (up to about 30 m) with a smooth, slender trunk and a crown of long (4–6 m) feather leaves over clusters of large nuts, in groves or lining a beach road
- Points to: the North-east, which has 82% of Brazil's planted coconut area (Ceará, Bahia and Sergipe lead), mostly on the coast but also inland in the São Francisco valley of Pernambuco and Bahia (about 12,000 ha, around Petrolândia, Rodelas and Petrolina–Juazeiro); outside the region, Pará (10%, fourth among the states) and Espírito Santo (4%)
- Strength: weak
- Counterexamples: groves are farmed in 23 of the 27 federal units (all but Santa Catarina, Rio Grande do Sul, the Distrito Federal and Amapá) and the palm grows worldwide, so a few palms prove little
- Sources: IBGE, PAM 2025, planted area by state, mesoregion and municipality, https://sidra.ibge.gov.br/tabela/5457; Flora e Funga do Brasil via GBIF (recorded in the nine North-east states, accessed Nov 2026), https://api.gbif.org/v1/species/114690468/distributions; Plonkit Brazil guide (community, common across the North-east and sometimes along the coast beyond it, grows worldwide, updated 31 Oct 2026), https://www.plonkit.net/brazil; en.wikipedia "Coconut" (up to 30 m, pinnate leaves 4–6 m, smooth trunk, accessed Nov 2026), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coconut

### Pantanal: flooded plain with carandá palms
- Look for: dead-flat open grassland to the horizon with shallow lagoons or flooded fields in the local flood season, clusters or stands of fan palms with small bluish-green fans on slender, smooth grey trunks (carandá), and cattle; altitude cannot be seen in a photo, so check a DEM once there is a candidate (the plain lies at about 80–150 m)
- Points to: the Pantanal, in Mato Grosso do Sul (most of it) and Mato Grosso; carandá is native only in those two states
- Strength: weak on its own (vegetation and flooding change with the season); medium when the month is known and fits the local flood season (rain falls mostly November–March, but the flood moves slowly south: January–March around Cuiabá and Cáceres, April–July around Corumbá and Ladário, two or more months later towards Porto Murtinho, in the dry season) or with a second Mato Grosso do Sul or Mato Grosso clue
- Counterexamples: the same flooded plain continues into Bolivia and Paraguay, and carandá is common in the Chaco of Paraguay and Bolivia and in Argentina's Formosa province; imagery from outside the local flood season shows plain grassland; carnaúba stands on the seasonally flooded river plains of Piauí and Ceará look much the same, though their fans are larger (about 1.5 m against 40–70 cm, single source); Amapá's flooded coastal grasslands, grazed by cattle and buffalo, also hold fan palms, but these are buriti (single source), with a thick trunk (up to 70 cm) and a big round crown, and Marajó's flooded campos are grazing land too
- Sources: IBGE, Biomas e Sistema Costeiro-Marinho do Brasil, 2019 (Pantanal only in MT and MS, plain at 80–150 m, wet season, carandá common where water collects on saline soils), https://biblioteca.ibge.gov.br/visualizacao/livros/liv101676.pdf; Flora e Funga do Brasil via GBIF (carandá native in MS and MT, accessed Nov 2026), https://api.gbif.org/v1/species/114690473/distributions; Embrapa Pantanal, Jorge et al. 2014, carandá germination circular (widespread in the Paraguayan and Bolivian Chaco, in Brazil only MT and MS, bluish-green fan leaves), https://www.infoteca.cnptia.embrapa.br/infoteca/bitstream/doc/1009714/1/CT110.pdf; Embrapa Pantanal, Galdino & Resende (the flood moves north to south: January–March around Cuiabá and Cáceres, April–July around Corumbá and Ladário, later at Porto Murtinho), https://www.infoteca.cnptia.embrapa.br/bitstream/doc/812749/1/ADM004.pdf; en.wikipedia "Pantanal" (extends into Bolivia and Paraguay, about 80% of the floodplain under water in the rains, most rain November–March, 80–150 m, accessed Nov 2026), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantanal; en.wikipedia "Copernicia alba" (carandá in Argentina's Formosa province, smooth grey trunk, leaves 40–70 cm, accessed Nov 2026), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copernicia_alba; en.wikipedia "Copernicia prunifera" (carnaúba stands in flood zones and near rivers, accessed Nov 2026), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copernicia_prunifera; Costa 2008, Produção animal nos campos inundáveis, archived by Embrapa Ainfo (Amapá's flooded grasslands with buriti on permanently wet ground, cattle and buffalo, Marajó's campos as flooded pasture), https://ainfo.cnptia.embrapa.br/digital/bitstream/item/63695/1/AP-2008-producao-animal-campos-inundaveis.pdf; en.wikipedia "Mauritia flexuosa" (trunk up to 70 cm, large leaves in a rounded crown, accessed Nov 2026), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mauritia_flexuosa

### Pampa grassland
- Look for: open, almost treeless rolling or flat grassland (coxilhas) with trees only along streams, cattle, and soil that is not red
- Points to: the south and west of Rio Grande do Sul, from the coastal plain, the Serra do Sudeste and the Campanha to the Fronteira Oeste and parts of the Missões (the Pampa covers about 69% of the state)
- Strength: weak on its own (vegetation, soil colour included); medium together with a second Rio Grande do Sul clue from outside the landscape, such as a plate, an `ERS-` or `RSC-` road number or an RS distributor's name (clues.md)
- Counterexamples: similar natural grassland covers the southern plateau of Paraná, Santa Catarina and north-eastern Rio Grande do Sul, outside the Pampa biome (Campos Gerais in Paraná; Campos de Cima da Serra around Vacaria and Bom Jesus in Rio Grande do Sul, high ground with araucaria groves); the Pampa itself continues into Uruguay and Argentina
- Sources: IBGE, Biomas e Sistema Costeiro-Marinho do Brasil, 2019 (grass steppe with no dry season, trees on river banks, 68.8% of RS, steppe on the southern plateau from Paraná to Rio Grande do Sul placed in the Atlantic Forest biome), https://biblioteca.ibge.gov.br/visualizacao/livros/liv101676.pdf; pt.wikipedia "Pampa" (coxilhas, southern half of RS, continues into Uruguay and six Argentine provinces, plateau grasslands of PR, SC and northern RS, accessed Nov 2026), https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pampa; Embrapa Pecuária Sul, Documentos 167 (Pampa grassland in the Serra do Sudeste, Litoral Sul, Campanha, Fronteira Oeste and Missões), https://www.infoteca.cnptia.embrapa.br/infoteca/bitstream/doc/1132635/1/DT-167-online.pdf; pt.wikipedia "Campos de Cima da Serra" (far north-east of RS, high grassland with araucaria groves, accessed Nov 2026), https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Campos_de_Cima_da_Serra; Plonkit Brazil guide (community, soil not red in most of RS, southern areas resemble Uruguay, updated 31 Oct 2026), https://www.plonkit.net/brazil

## Soil

### Terra roxa: dark maroon soil
- Look for: dark red to dark reddish-brown clay in road cuts and ploughed fields, a deeper, darker red (maroon) than the lighter red, pinkish or orange soils common elsewhere; on basalt at Londrina the moist topsoil is about Munsell 2.5YR 3/3 over a 10R 3/6 subsoil
- Points to: mainly the Paraná-basin basalt plateau: northern and western Paraná (e.g. Londrina), though in the Caiuá-sandstone north-west only where valleys such as the Pirapó and Ivaí cut down to the basalt (single source); western Santa Catarina; northern Rio Grande do Sul (e.g. Passo Fundo); São Paulo around Ribeirão Preto and Jaú rather than the sandstone western plateau, where basalt shows mainly in the lower river valleys; on the cold southern uplands, mostly above about 800 m, the same volcanic rock also gives brownish (bruno) soils (single source); also secondary areas in Mato Grosso do Sul, Goiás, Minas Gerais, Mato Grosso and Tocantins, and small patches around Altamira (Pará), e.g. about 100 km along the Transamazônica towards Itaituba
- Strength: medium for deep maroon soil; weak for "red" alone
- Counterexamples: strongly red latosols are widespread in the Centro-Oeste, South and South-east, so plain red soil proves little; the same soil continues into Misiones and Corrientes (Argentina) and parts of Paraguay; maroon soil does not rule out the North or North-east: besides the Altamira patches, a similar dark reddish Nitossolo has developed on basalt at Cabo de Santo Agostinho on the Pernambuco coast (single source), even though yellow latosols prevail on the Barreiras tablelands of the coast and on the low Amazon plateaus

## Crops and plantations

### Pine and eucalyptus plantations
- Look for: dense, even-aged plantations of pine (conifers) or eucalyptus set out in straight rows
- Points to: pine → the South: Paraná, Santa Catarina (the largest share of its own land) and Rio Grande do Sul hold about 87% of planted pine, then São Paulo; eucalyptus → no region in particular (largest areas in Minas Gerais, Mato Grosso do Sul and São Paulo, densest in Mato Grosso do Sul and Espírito Santo)
- Strength: weak alone (vegetation); medium for pine with a second clue pointing to the South; eucalyptus stays weak
- Counterexamples: São Paulo and Minas Gerais hold about 10–12% of planted pine, and tropical P. caribaea has been planted commercially in the South-east and Centre-West (single source); elsewhere pine is rare, about 1% of the area across the Centre-West, North and North-east together (small stands in Rondônia, Mato Grosso do Sul and Goiás, almost none in the North-east)
- Sources: IBGE, PEVS 2025 (planted pine and eucalyptus area by state, 2025 data), https://sidra.ibge.gov.br/tabela/5930; Indústria Brasileira de Árvores, annual report 2026 (about 89% of pine area in the South, 10% in the South-east and 1% elsewhere, eucalyptus led by Minas Gerais, Mato Grosso do Sul and São Paulo, 2025 data), https://iba.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/IBA_relatorio_2026_digital.pdf; Embrapa, Cultivo de Pinus (commercial pine mainly in the South and South-east, tropical P. caribaea spreading into the South-east and Centre-West, 2011 text reissued 2014), https://www.infoteca.cnptia.embrapa.br/infoteca/bitstream/doc/1155568/1/EmbrapaCultivoDePinus2014.pdf

### Sugarcane
- Look for: tall dense cane fields, often reaching the roadside, with harvest machines and cane trucks, or burnt fields and hand-cutting crews
- Points to: São Paulo state (53% of Brazil's cane area, a fifth of the state's land), then Minas Gerais, Goiás, Mato Grosso do Sul and Paraná; in the North-east, the coastal Zona da Mata of Alagoas and Pernambuco. Burnt cane and hand-cutting crews point to the North-east, where 73% of cane is still cut by hand (traditionally after burning), not to São Paulo, now over 99% machine-cut (63% in 2010/11) after a 2007 state agreement to phase out burning. Harvest runs April–December in São Paulo and roughly August–March on the coast from Alagoas to Rio Grande do Norte, so harvesting in January–February points to that coast; March is ambiguous, as Centre-South mills restart in March–April
- Strength: weak alone (vegetation); medium when cane is being harvested in a month only one belt harvests (January–February: the coast from Alagoas to Rio Grande do Norte; April–June: anywhere but that coast), or when burnt cane is being cut by hand
- Counterexamples: cane is grown in small plots in most states; a few Centre-South mills still crush in January (some in south-eastern Mato Grosso do Sul run a continuous season); Rio de Janeiro state also cuts most of its cane by hand (84%); wildfires still burn cane in São Paulo (over 181,000 ha of cane and regrowth between 23 August and 10 September 2024), so a burnt field without hand-cutting crews is no North-east clue
- Sources: IBGE, PAM 2025 (cane area by state, 2025 data), https://sidra.ibge.gov.br/tabela/5457; CONAB, sugarcane crop survey (areas by region, harvest calendars, manual and machine harvest shares by state, April 2026), https://www.gov.br/conab/pt-br/atuacao/informacoes-agropecuarias/safras/safra-de-cana-de-acucar/arquivos-boletins/1o-levantamento-safra-2026-27/e-book_boletim-de-safras-cana_1o-lev-2026-27.pdf; UNICA harvest bulletin, hosted by NovaCana (Centre-South between harvests in January with few mills crushing, restart mainly from the second half of March, 6 Feb 2026), https://www.novacana.com/pdf/06022026110212_Unica_-_060226_NC.pdf; Revista Cultivar citing Orplana (over 181,000 ha of São Paulo cane and regrowth burned, 23 August–10 September 2024; 13 Sep 2024), https://www.revistacultivar.com.br/noticias/queimadas-devastam-mais-de-181-mil-hectares-de-canaviais-e-areas-de-rebrota-no-estado-de-sao-paulo; ESALQ/USP, Visão Agrícola no. 8 (São Paulo's 2007 agreement to end cane burning by 2014 on gentle slopes and by 2017 on steeper ones, 2008), https://www.esalq.usp.br/visaoagricola/sites/default/files/VA08-ambiente03.pdf; Plonkit Brazil guide (community, cane most common in São Paulo and also in Paraná, Mato Grosso do Sul and the north-eastern coast, checked Nov 2026), https://www.plonkit.net/brazil

### Coffee
- Look for: rows of coffee shrubs on hillsides or on flat plateaus
- Points to: Minas Gerais (56% of coffee area), Espírito Santo (22%, mostly conilon, about a tenth of the state's land), São Paulo (10%, mainly the Mogiana in the north-east, around Franca and along the Minas border, plus the Alta Paulista around Marília and Garça and the Circuito das Águas around Amparo), then Bahia (arabica on the Planalto and in the far west, conilon in the south) and Rondônia (conilon only)
- Strength: weak alone (vegetation); medium with a second clue pointing to one of these states
- Counterexamples: smaller plantings in Paraná, Rio de Janeiro and elsewhere
- Sources: IBGE, PAM 2025 (coffee area by state, arabica and canephora, 2025 data), https://sidra.ibge.gov.br/tabela/5457; CONAB, coffee crop survey (arabica and conilon by state, the producing regions of São Paulo and Bahia, harvest season, May 2026), https://www.gov.br/conab/pt-br/atuacao/informacoes-agropecuarias/safras/safra-de-cafe/2o-levantamento-de-cafe-safra-2026/boletim-de-safras-cafe-mai_26.pdf; Plonkit Brazil guide (community, coffee mostly in Minas Gerais, also Espírito Santo and north-eastern São Paulo, checked Nov 2026), https://www.plonkit.net/brazil

### Orange groves
- Look for: large blocks of small, evenly spaced evergreen trees in long rows, divided by service tracks; round orange or green fruit in the crowns (ripe oranges can stay green in hot weather) marks them as citrus
- Points to: São Paulo state (61% of orange area, three-quarters of output) and the Triângulo and south-west of Minas Gerais, the "citrus belt"; a second, smaller pole on the coastal tablelands of Sergipe and the north coast of Bahia (around Rio Real), with Bahia and Sergipe together holding about 15% of orange area
- Strength: weak alone (vegetation); medium with a second clue pointing to one of these areas
- Counterexamples: smaller groves in Paraná, Rio Grande do Sul and Pará (about 3–4% of orange area each), and small orchards in most other states
- Sources: IBGE, PAM 2025 (orange area and output by state, 2025 data), https://sidra.ibge.gov.br/tabela/5457; Fundecitrus, citrus belt inventory 2025–2026 (belt limits, trees planted at set spacing in blocks divided by roads and service tracks, 2025), https://www.fundecitrus.com.br/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/2025_06_10_Inventario-e-Estimativa-do-Cinturao-Citricola-2025-2026.pdf; Embrapa Tabuleiros Costeiros, Comunicado Técnico 246 (the Bahia–Sergipe coastal-tablelands pole is the North-east's main orange area, Rio Real the top producer on the Bahia north coast, Dec 2021), https://www.infoteca.cnptia.embrapa.br/infoteca/bitstream/doc/1138062/1/COT-246-21-Embrapa-Tabuleiros-Costeiros-1.pdf; en.wikipedia article on the orange fruit (a small evergreen tree, ripe fruit can stay green in warm climates, revision of Nov 2026), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orange_%28fruit%29

### Irrigated rice paddies
- Look for: flat, flooded or levee-divided fields of rice
- Points to: Rio Grande do Sul (over half of Brazil's rice area, about 70% of output, mostly irrigated), then Santa Catarina; also the irrigated floodplains of the Formoso, Javaés and Araguaia rivers in south-west Tocantins (around Lagoa da Confusão and Formoso do Araguaia), with about 125,000 ha of irrigated rice in 2023/24
- Strength: weak alone (vegetation); medium with a second clue pointing to the South; a month in the southern season (sown September–November, harvested February–May) only checks consistency, since Tocantins sows its irrigated rice between 10 October and 20 December
- Counterexamples: smaller irrigated rice areas in Roraima, Pará (Marajó), north-west Minas Gerais, north-west Paraná and parts of the North-east; rice without standing water may be rain-fed upland rice, as in Mato Grosso (the second-largest rice area), Maranhão and Piauí
- Sources: IBGE, PAM 2025 (rice area and output by state, 2025 data), https://sidra.ibge.gov.br/tabela/5457; Rio Grande do Sul government (about 70% of national rice, irrigated, 14 June 2024), https://www.estado.rs.gov.br/colheita-de-arroz-se-encerra-no-rs-com-7-16-milhoes-de-toneladas-do-grao; Embrapa Arroz e Feijão, Circular Técnica 95 (Tocantins irrigated rice, 125,300 ha in 2023/24 on the Formoso, Javaés and Araguaia floodplains, Oct 2025), https://www.infoteca.cnptia.embrapa.br/infoteca/bitstream/doc/1180686/1/37343.pdf; CONAB, grain crop survey (irrigated and upland rice by region, Rio Grande do Sul sowing and harvest months, July 2026), https://www.gov.br/conab/pt-br/atuacao/informacoes-agropecuarias/safras/safra-de-graos/boletim-da-safra-de-graos/10o-levantamento-safra-2025-26/e-book_boletim-de-safras-10o-levantamento_2026.pdf

### Apple orchards and vineyards
- Look for: apple orchards in cool highlands, increasingly under white or black hail nets; vineyards on trellises, mostly overhead pergolas (latada), in Rio Grande do Sul on small family plots
- Points to: apples → Santa Catarina and Rio Grande do Sul (about 96% of the area), in the Planalto Serrano and the Campos de Cima da Serra; vines → Rio Grande do Sul (56% of vine area), about four-fifths of it in the Serra Gaúcha around Caxias do Sul (single source), or the irrigated São Francisco valley (Pernambuco and Bahia), where vines stand in Caatinga country and give more than one harvest a year
- Strength: weak alone (vegetation); medium with a second clue to the region that is not landscape (a plate, road number or distributor pointing to Santa Catarina and Rio Grande do Sul, or to Pernambuco and Bahia)
- Counterexamples: smaller apple and grape areas in Paraná, São Paulo and Minas Gerais; the Caatinga around the São Francisco vineyards is leafless in the dry season but leafs out with the November–April rains (new leaves mainly January–May; single source each), so a green background does not rule that valley out; overhead pergolas are also the usual trellis for table grapes in the São Francisco valley, northern Paraná, western São Paulo and northern Minas Gerais, so the trellis type does not tell the regions apart
- Sources: IBGE, PAM 2025 (apple and grape area by state, 2025 data), https://sidra.ibge.gov.br/tabela/5457; Embrapa, apple chapter of the Anuário HF 2026 (Planalto Serrano and Campos de Cima da Serra, Santa Catarina and Rio Grande do Sul about 97% of the harvest, 2026), https://www.infoteca.cnptia.embrapa.br/infoteca/bitstream/doc/1185986/1/HAwerroth-Anuario-HF-2026.pdf; Agência FAPESP (white and black hail nets spreading fast over apple orchards around Vacaria, 11 Aug 2026), https://agencia.fapesp.br/58935; Embrapa, Cadastro Vitícola do Rio Grande do Sul (Caxias do Sul microregion of the Serra Gaúcha 80% of the state's vineyard area, overhead latada trellis most used, 2.8 ha of vines per farm on average, 2015 data published 2017), https://www.alice.cnptia.embrapa.br/alice/bitstream/doc/1084013/1/DadoscadastraisCAP1ebookA45.pdf; Embrapa Semiárido, Circular Técnica 80 (latada used for table grapes in the São Francisco valley and traditional in the Serra Gaúcha, northern Paraná, west and north-west São Paulo and northern Minas Gerais), https://www.infoteca.cnptia.embrapa.br/infoteca/bitstream/doc/156929/1/CTE80.pdf; Embrapa Uva e Vinho, Comunicado Técnico 226 (São Francisco valley vines in Pernambuco and Bahia, up to 2.5 harvests a year with irrigation, Dec 2022), https://www.infoteca.cnptia.embrapa.br/infoteca/bitstream/doc/1149674/1/Com-Tec-226.pdf; Embrapa Semiárido, agrometeorology of the Petrolina–Juazeiro pole (90% of the rain falls November–April, 2011), https://www.infoteca.cnptia.embrapa.br/infoteca/bitstream/doc/896998/1/Agrometeorologia.pdf; Embrapa Semiárido, caatinga phenology at Petrolina (species leafless in the dry season, new leaves mainly January–May, 2017 paper on 2016 data), https://www.alice.cnptia.embrapa.br/alice/bitstream/doc/1083575/1/Magna7.pdf; en.wikipedia "Brazilian wine" (Serra Gaúcha the main wine region, São Francisco valley hot and semi-arid with two crops a year, revision of Jun 2026), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brazilian_wine

### Soy and maize fields
- Look for: vast flat fields with grain silos and big farm machinery; soy grows as knee-high rows of broad-leaved bushy plants that yellow and then brown before harvest, maize as head-high grass-like stalks with tassels and cobs
- Points to: soy → Mato Grosso above all (over a quarter of soy area), then Rio Grande do Sul, Paraná, Goiás and Mato Grosso do Sul, and the northern frontier: the MATOPIBA states (Maranhão, Tocantins, Piauí, far-western Bahia) and Pará; maize → Mato Grosso (32% of maize area), then Paraná, Goiás and Mato Grosso do Sul, with Rio Grande do Sul only about 3%
- Strength: weak (spread over many states, and seasonal)
- Counterexamples: soy grows in 24 of 27 states (none in Paraíba, Pernambuco or Sergipe); maize grows in every state, including about 580,000 ha in Ceará and a third-crop belt in the Sertão and Agreste of Sergipe and north-east Bahia, sown in April–May
- Sources: IBGE, PAM 2025 (soy and maize area by state, 2025 data), https://sidra.ibge.gov.br/tabela/5457; CONAB, grain crop survey (far-western Bahia soy, third-crop maize regions and sowing months, July 2026), https://www.gov.br/conab/pt-br/atuacao/informacoes-agropecuarias/safras/safra-de-graos/boletim-da-safra-de-graos/10o-levantamento-safra-2025-26/e-book_boletim-de-safras-10o-levantamento_2026.pdf; Reuters via Notícias Agrícolas, on CONAB's 2025/26 outlook (Mato Grosso 13.1 of 49.1 million ha of soy, growth in Pará, Bahia, Tocantins and Maranhão, 18 Sep 2025), https://www.noticiasagricolas.com.br/noticias/soja/407786-conab-ve-aumento-de-37-no-plantio-de-soja-no-brasil-em-202526-e-nova-safra-recorde.html; Plonkit Brazil guide (community, soy most common in Mato Grosso, Paraná and Goiás, checked Nov 2026), https://www.plonkit.net/brazil
